Phase A

What's live today, and what it's costing you.

The site runs the Start Bootstrap "Creative" theme on Jekyll. It's not broken — it's generic, and in two places it actively undermines you.

Critical · costing you traffic right now
https://www.smashglass.com throws a browser security warning

Your brief refers to the site as www.smashglass.com, which is almost certainly the URL on your Upwork profile, your LinkedIn, and your email signature. That address is served over a GitHub wildcard certificate for *.github.io, which does not cover your domain. Every visitor who uses it gets a full-page red interstitial — ERR_CERT_COMMON_NAME_INVALID — before they see a single word you wrote. Most of them leave there.

Only the bare apex, smashglass.com, has a valid certificate. The cause is that your CNAME file names the apex only, so GitHub never provisioned a certificate for the www host, even though DNS points www at Pages.

Fix: set the Pages custom domain to www.smashglass.com → GitHub provisions both certs and redirects apex → www

Roughly five minutes, and it is worth more than every other recommendation in this document combined.

The headline is "Helping improve our world one project at a time!" — a sentiment, not a position. A founder landing here from Upwork learns nothing about what you do in the first five seconds. The body copy underneath commits hard to the automotive aftermarket (Service Dealer, Estimation, Part Ordering), which is a real credential but a narrow one, and it contradicts the AI-agent studio you're trying to sell.

The three service cards — Automotive, Custom Software, Integration Services — describe categories, not problems. There is no process, no point of view, no evidence of engineering judgment. The only call to action is a bare mailto:, which is the highest-friction conversion path available and gives you zero qualification data.

Reference check

simon.ai isn't the site you remember — and that's useful.

Your brief describes it as cinematic animated backgrounds with depth and dimensionality. I pulled the page. It's something else, and what it actually is points somewhere better for you.

Simon AI is an agentic marketing platform, and the site is a Webflow build running GSAP and Rive — no Three.js, no WebGL, no 3D scene anywhere on the page. The motion that reads as expensive is Rive vector animation of product UI, sequenced with GSAP. It's a clean, confident B2B SaaS page, not a cinematic experience.

The hero is not a symbol. It's a chat prompt — "What can I help you with today?" — with an animated cursor and suggested actions, i.e. the product doing the job you'd hire it for. Below that: a live email preview being shaped by customer data, a breakdown of data signals, three platform components, then logos and testimonials.

So two things follow. First, the Webflow runtime you ruled out is exactly what's producing that polish, which means copying the feel isn't the goal — the underlying move is. Second, and more importantly: the thing that converts on that page is legibility, not spectacle. A visitor understands what they'd be buying in about four seconds, because they're watching it work.

Much of their page is carried by customer logos and testimonials — which you've correctly ruled out, since you don't have publishable ones yet. That makes the demonstration carry more weight for you than it does for them, not less. It's the only proof you have.

What I'd take from it
  • Show the work, don't symbolize it. A hero that demonstrates an agent running beats a beautiful abstract object for a buyer deciding whether to send money.
  • Motion serves the demo. Every animation on their page explains something. None of it is ambient.
  • Restraint reads as expensive. Generous whitespace, tight type, one accent.
What I'd leave
  • The logo carousel and testimonial wall. You have none, and fabricating them is off the table.
  • The Webflow runtime. You ruled it out, correctly — it's a hosting dependency you don't need.
  • The generic SaaS gradient sections. This is where their page is weakest and where you can clearly beat it.

Directions 01–03 below are the cinematic options your brief asked for, and they're real — I'd build any of them. Direction 04 is the one this reference check actually points at, and you should read it before you choose.

The spine

The name is the asset you aren't using.

Every direction below is built on the same observation: "SmashGlass" is a better brand than the site gives it credit for. The current design could belong to any consultancy; swap the wordmark and nothing changes. That's the actual failure.

Glass is unusually generous as a visual system. It refracts — one undifferentiated input enters, ordered outputs leave, which is a literal picture of "transforming complexity into clarity." It is a barrier — the pane between a business and the system it wishes it had. It is a lattice — a material that is rigid because of how its components are bonded. And it breaks, which is the verb in your name and the thing you do to problems.

Three directions, three readings of the same material. All dark-first, all built on the same four service lines, all deliverable on GitHub Pages. They diverge on what the central 3D object means — and that choice sets the tone of the entire brand.

Build 04's hero. Dress it in 01's identity.

04+01

Before I looked at the reference site I would have said Refraction, straight. Having looked at it, I'd change the order: lead with the running agent, and spend the visual identity everywhere else.

The hero's job is to convert a founder who arrived from Upwork with thirty seconds of patience. A prism asks them to infer what you do. A console shows them. Direction 04 wins that fight, and it wins it on mobile, which is where a meaningful share of that traffic actually lands.

But 04 alone is forgettable — it looks like a dev tool. So Refraction supplies everything around it: the prism becomes the wordmark and the section-transition motif, the spectrum becomes the four service colors, and the dispersion scene takes over as the full-bleed moment behind What We Build — where "one input, four outputs" is literally the content.

How the pieces land

Hero — 04's agent console, left-aligned headline, no 3D above the fold. Fast first paint, legible in four seconds, full copy in the initial HTML.

What We Build — Refraction's prism, full-bleed. One beam in, four rays out, each terminating at a service card in its own spectrum color. The graphic and the information are the same object.

How We Work — Lattice's point-and-line engine, scroll-driven, assembling across Discover → Architect → Build → Validate → Deliver.

Capabilities — the 04 console again, three scripts, each labeled a demonstration.

And build the renderers in that order. 04 needs no WebGL at all, so the site is live and converting before a single Three.js byte ships. The prism and the lattice land as progressive enhancements on hardware that can afford them. That also gives your reduced-motion and low-end-mobile path for free — it is just the site minus the enhancements, with no content lost.

On type: Instrument Serif for the hero headline only, Instrument Sans for every other heading. Editorial where it earns attention, engineered everywhere it has to do work.

Phase D preview

How this gets built, and the two things blocking it.

Your brief proposed React + Vite + Tailwind + R3F. I'd change one thing in that stack, for a reason that matters commercially.

A Vite React SPA on GitHub Pages ships an empty <div id="root"> and renders everything client-side. For a site whose top two jobs are search discoverability and fast first paint from an Upwork or LinkedIn click, that's the wrong default — you'd be paying a rendering tax on 90% of the page to support the 10% that's actually interactive.

I'd use Astro with React islands instead. Same React components, same Tailwind, same R3F — but Astro ships zero JavaScript by default and hydrates only the WebGL hero and the inquiry form. Every word of your copy is in the initial HTML for crawlers and link previewers. If you'd rather stay on pure React, vite-react-ssg gets you most of the way by prerendering at build time; Astro just makes the island boundary explicit instead of something you have to remember to maintain.

FrameworkAstro 5 + React islands

Zero JS baseline, real HTML for SEO, React only where it earns its weight.

StylingTailwind + CSS tokens

As you proposed. Design tokens as CSS custom properties so themes and tiers stay swappable.

3DReact Three Fiber + drei

Loaded as a progressive enhancement below the fold, never in the hero. Three fidelity tiers chosen at runtime from device capability.

AnimationMotion + native scroll-driven CSS

Scroll-linked animation in CSS where supported, Motion for orchestration. This is the layer doing what GSAP and Rive do on the reference site.

Inquiry formFormspree or Basin, + Cal.com

Project type, business problem, budget band, contact preference. Four fields, honeypot, no backend. Cal.com for the discovery-call path.

DeployGitHub Actions → Pages

01

The www certificate, before anything else. This is independent of the redesign and shouldn't wait for it. Setting the Pages custom domain to www.smashglass.com makes GitHub provision certificates for both hosts and redirect the apex to www. Until that's done, every link you've already handed out is broken.

02

Your gh token has no workflow scope. Current scopes are admin:public_key, gist, read:org, repo — which means any push that creates or edits .github/workflows/deploy.yml will be rejected. Fix it before we start building: gh auth refresh -h github.com -s workflow.

03

Pages is on the legacy Jekyll builder. The repo currently reports build_type: legacy with source master /, meaning GitHub builds Jekyll for you automatically. That has to flip to workflow before an Actions-built site can deploy. It's a one-time setting change in repo settings and I'll need you to confirm before I touch it, since it's the switch that takes the current site down.

04

Nothing in the proposed copy claims anything you can't back. No clients, no testimonials, no metrics, no team. The "Featured Capabilities" section ships as clearly labeled interactive demonstrations — an ops agent, an AI-assisted engineering workflow, an integration pipeline — not as case studies, until you have real work you're free to publish.
